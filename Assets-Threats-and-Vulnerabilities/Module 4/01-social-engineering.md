# Social Engineering

**Social Engineering:** A manipulation technique that exploits human error to gain private information, access, or valuables.

Social engineering attacks are popular among **threat actors** because it is often easier to trick people into providing access, information, or money than to exploit a software or network vulnerability.

Social engineering is an **umbrella term** that covers a broad range of attacks. These techniques take advantage of people's:

- Trust
- Curiosity
- Generosity
- Excitement
- Willingness to help

# Social Engineering Risks

Social engineering is a form of **deception** that takes advantage of the way people think and make decisions.

Threat actors manipulate people's natural feelings and use them against their targets. These attacks can be especially harmful because they often:

- Are relatively easy to perform.
- Do not require advanced technical skills.
- Can bypass technological security defenses.
- Can affect anyone, from entry-level employees to senior executives.

## Example: Twitter Hack of 2020

In the **[Twitter Hack of 2020](https://www.dfs.ny.gov/Twitter_Report)**, a group of hackers called Twitter employees while pretending to be members of the IT department.

Using this social engineering technique, the attackers:

- Gained access to Twitter's network and internal tools.
- Took control of accounts belonging to high-profile users.
- Targeted politicians, celebrities, entrepreneurs, and others.

This incident demonstrates that even **basic social engineering techniques** can create significant security problems.

## Defending Against Social Engineering

Defending against social engineering requires a **multi-layered approach** that combines:

- **Technological security controls**
- **User awareness**
- **Security training**

# Signs and Types of Social Engineering Attacks

Recognizing the signs of social engineering is important because people may not realize an attack is occurring until it is too late.

Common types of social engineering include:

## 1. Baiting

**Baiting:** A social engineering tactic that tempts people into compromising their security.

### Example

**USB baiting** involves an attacker leaving an infected USB drive where someone may find it.

If the person plugs the USB drive into their device, malicious software may be installed or executed.

## 2. Phishing

**Phishing:** The use of digital communications to trick people into revealing sensitive information or deploying malicious software.

Phishing is one of the **most common forms of social engineering** and is typically performed through:

- Email
- Malicious links
- Malicious attachments
- Other digital communications

## 3. Quid Pro Quo

**Quid Pro Quo:** A type of baiting that tricks someone into believing they will receive a reward in exchange for sharing access, information, or money.

### Example

An attacker may pretend to be a **loan officer at a bank** and contact customers with an offer for a lower credit card interest rate.

The attacker may claim that the customer only needs to provide their account details to receive the offer.

The victim is tricked into providing sensitive information because they expect something in return.

## 4. Tailgating

**Tailgating:** A social engineering tactic in which an unauthorized person follows an authorized person into a restricted area.

Tailgating is also sometimes called **piggybacking**.

### Example

An unauthorized person may wait for an employee to open a secured door and then follow them inside without having their own authorization.

## 5. Watering Hole

**Watering Hole:** An attack in which a threat actor compromises a website frequently visited by a specific group of users.

These websites are often infected with **malicious software** so that visitors can become targets of the attack.

### Example: Holy Water Attack of 2020

The **Holy Water attack of 2020** infected various:

- Religious websites
- Charity websites
- Volunteer websites

The attackers targeted websites that were frequently visited by particular groups of users.

# Social Engineering Awareness

Everyone can be vulnerable to social engineering attacks, regardless of their position in an organization.

Security awareness training is an important way to reduce these risks.

There are three main areas to focus on:

## 1. Stay Alert

Be alert when dealing with:

- Suspicious communications
- Unknown people
- Unexpected emails
- Unusual requests

When dealing with email:

- Look for **spelling errors**.
- Check the **sender's name**.
- Verify the **sender's email address**.
- Be cautious with unexpected links and attachments.

## 2. Be Cautious About Sharing Information

Be careful about sharing personal or organizational information, especially on **social media**.

Threat actors may search social media platforms for information that can be used to:

- Impersonate someone.
- Build trust with a target.
- Create convincing scams.
- Gain unauthorized access.

## 3. Control Curiosity

Be cautious when something seems **too good to be true**.

Avoid immediately clicking:

- Unexpected email attachments
- Suspicious links
- Online advertisements
- Offers that seem unusually attractive

Attackers often use curiosity and excitement to encourage victims to take unsafe actions.

# Security Controls Against Social Engineering

Technology can provide additional layers of defense if someone makes a mistake.

Examples of security controls include:

- **Firewalls**
- **Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)**
- **Block lists**
- **Email filtering**
- Other security technologies

These controls help create a **layered defense** against social engineering attacks.

# Security Training

Security awareness should extend beyond employees.

Organizations can also educate:

- **Customers**
- **Employees**
- **Managers**
- **Executives**
- Other users who interact with organizational systems

Security analysts play an important role in promoting safe practices.

Examples of an analyst's responsibilities include:

- Testing systems.
- Identifying security weaknesses.
- Documenting best practices.
- Helping others understand security threats.
- Promoting safer security practices within the organization.

# Social Engineering Types at a Glance

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Baiting** | Tempts someone into compromising security | Infected USB drive |
| **Phishing** | Uses digital communication to steal information or deploy malware | Fake email requesting credentials |
| **Quid Pro Quo** | Promises a reward in exchange for information, access, or money | Fake bank offer |
| **Tailgating** | Unauthorized person follows an authorized person into a restricted area | Following an employee through a secure door |
| **Watering Hole** | Compromises a website frequently visited by a target group | Infected charity or religious website |

# Key Takeaways

- **Social engineering** exploits **human error and trust** rather than only technical vulnerabilities.
- Attackers use emotions such as **curiosity, generosity, and excitement** to manipulate people.
- Social engineering attacks can be performed without advanced technical skills.
- Common social engineering techniques include:
  - **Baiting**
  - **Phishing**
  - **Quid pro quo**
  - **Tailgating**
  - **Watering hole**
- **Security awareness and training** are important defenses.
- Users should **stay alert**, **be cautious about sharing information**, and **control curiosity**.
- Technologies such as **firewalls, MFA, block lists, and email filtering** provide additional layers of defense.
- Security awareness should include **employees and customers**.
- Recognizing the signs of social engineering and using effective security controls can significantly reduce the risk of successful attacks.
