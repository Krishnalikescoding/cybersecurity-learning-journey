# Security Plans

## Overview

Security depends on three core elements:

- **People**
- **Processes**
- **Technology**

Security is a team effort that extends beyond the IT department. It is an organizational **culture**, consisting of shared values that span all levels of an organization.

These values involve:

- Employees
- Vendors
- Customers

Protecting digital and physical assets requires participation from everyone in the organization.

## Security Culture

A strong security culture ensures that everyone understands their role in protecting organizational assets.

Security plans help organizations prepare for risks and respond effectively when they occur.

Effective security plans should:

- Focus on people.
- Consider diverse backgrounds and perspectives.
- Ensure that everyone involved understands their responsibilities.
- Address risks that could affect organizational assets.

## Security Risks

A **risk** is anything that can impact the **confidentiality, integrity, or availability (CIA)** of an asset.

Security plans commonly organize risks according to categories and contributing factors.

### Common Risk Categories

Examples of risk categories include:

- **Damage** to information
- **Disclosure** of information
- **Loss** of information

### Factors That Can Cause Risk

Risks can result from factors such as:

- Physical damage to a device
- Device malfunctions
- Cyberattacks
- Human error

### Example: Human Error

A new school teacher may be required to sign an agreement before beginning work. The agreement could warn employees about risks caused by human error, such as using a personal email account to send sensitive information.

A security plan may require all new employees to acknowledge and sign this agreement. This helps communicate the organization's security expectations and ensures employees understand their responsibilities.

The types of risks and factors addressed by a security plan vary depending on the organization.

## Elements of a Security Plan

Security plans consist of three basic elements:

1. **Policies**
2. **Standards**
3. **Procedures**

These elements provide the structure through which organizations communicate and implement their security plans.

## Policies

**Policy:** A set of rules designed to reduce risk and protect information.

Policies are the **foundation of a security plan**. They provide guidance to people inside and outside an organization by addressing questions such as:

- What are we protecting?
- Why are we protecting it?

Policies focus on the **strategic** side of security by defining:

- Scope
- Objectives
- Limitations

### Acceptable Use Policy

An **Acceptable Use Policy (AUP)** outlines secure and appropriate ways employees may access and use corporate systems.

New employees at many organizations are required to acknowledge or sign an AUP.

## Standards

**Standard:** A reference that informs how security policies should be established and how well assets should be protected.

Standards have a **tactical** function. They provide a point of reference that organizations can use when developing or improving security policies.

### Example: NIST Special Publication 800-63B

Organizations may use password management standards identified in **NIST Special Publication 800-63B** to inform their security policies.

The provided material gives the example of using such standards to specify requirements for employee passwords.

> **Note:** Standards serve as references for developing and improving security policies.

## Procedures

**Procedure:** Step-by-step instructions for performing a specific security task.

Organizations typically maintain multiple procedure documents covering security-related tasks.

Examples include procedures for:

- Choosing secure passwords
- Securely resetting a locked password

Clear and actionable procedures help create:

- **Accountability**
- **Consistency**
- **Efficiency**

across an organization.

## Policies vs Standards vs Procedures

| Element | Function | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Policy** | Strategic | Defines rules, scope, objectives, and limitations for reducing risk and protecting information. |
| **Standard** | Tactical | Provides references and points of reference for establishing and improving security policies. |
| **Procedure** | Operational | Provides step-by-step instructions for performing specific security tasks. |

## How the Elements Work Together

The three elements form a structured approach to security planning:

1. **Policies** establish the organization's security rules and strategic direction.
2. **Standards** provide references for determining how security should be implemented.
3. **Procedures** provide specific steps for carrying out security tasks.

These elements vary between organizations because security plans must be tailored to each organization's goals and requirements.

## Key Takeaways

- Security depends on **people, processes, and technology**.
- Security is an organizational **culture**, not solely an IT responsibility.
- Security plans help organizations prepare for and respond to risks.
- Common risk categories include **damage, disclosure, and loss of information**.
- Risks can result from physical damage, device malfunctions, attacks, and human error.
- The three basic elements of a security plan are **policies, standards, and procedures**.
- **Policies** provide strategic rules and form the foundation of a security plan.
- **Standards** provide references that inform security policies.
- **Procedures** provide step-by-step instructions for specific security tasks.
- Understanding the structure of security plans helps security professionals support an organization's security goals.
