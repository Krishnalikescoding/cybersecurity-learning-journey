# Security Compliance and NIST Cybersecurity Framework

## Overview

Having a security plan is only part of securing organizational assets. Organizations must also ensure that people follow the established requirements. This is known as **compliance**.

**Compliance:** The process of adhering to internal standards and external regulations.

Compliance is important for organizations of all sizes because it helps maintain:

- Trust
- Reputation
- Safety
- Data integrity

Failure to comply can also result in:

- Fines
- Penalties
- Lawsuits
- Financial damage
- Reputational damage

Compliance is particularly important in highly regulated industries such as:

- Healthcare
- Energy
- Finance

## Regulations

**Regulations:** Rules established by a government or other authority to control how something is done.

Like organizational policies, regulations are designed to protect people and their information, but they apply on a broader scale.

> **Note:** Regulations are requirements that must be followed, while security frameworks are resources that organizations can choose to use.

Because regulations vary around the world, maintaining compliance can be complex.

## NIST Cybersecurity Framework

The **National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST)** provides organizations with frameworks and security standards that reflect important security practices and regulations.

The **NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)** is a voluntary framework consisting of:

- Standards
- Guidelines
- Best practices

It is designed to help organizations manage **cybersecurity risk** and protect important assets, particularly information.

The NIST CSF is flexible and can be applied across different industries and organizations.

## Origins of the NIST CSF

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework was originally released in **2014** to help protect critical infrastructure in the United States.

NIST was selected to develop the framework because it provides an unbiased source of scientific data and practices.

The framework was later adapted to meet the needs of businesses in both the public and private sectors.

A major goal was to make the framework flexible enough for:

- Large organizations
- Small businesses
- Organizations with limited resources
- Organizations developing security plans

## Components of the NIST CSF

The NIST CSF consists of three main components:

1. **Core**
2. **Tiers**
3. **Profiles**

> **Note:** The core, tiers, and profiles work together to help organizations improve their security operations. The complete framework contains additional subcategories and processes.

## Core

The **CSF Core** is a set of desired cybersecurity outcomes that organizations can use to customize their security plans.

The current CSF Core consists of six functions:

1. **Identify**
2. **Protect**
3. **Detect**
4. **Respond**
5. **Recover**
6. **Govern**

These functions help organizations:

- Identify important assets.
- Protect assets using appropriate safeguards.
- Detect cybersecurity attacks and events.
- Develop response plans.
- Develop recovery plans.
- Address leadership and decision-making related to cybersecurity risk.

### The Govern Function

Originally, the CSF Core contained five functions.

The **Govern** function was added in **February 2024** to emphasize the importance of leadership and decision-making in managing cybersecurity risks.

## Tiers

**CSF Tiers** provide a way to measure the sophistication of an organization's cybersecurity program.

Tiers are measured on a scale from **1 to 4**.

| Tier | Description |
|---|---|
| **Tier 1** | The lowest level. Indicates that a limited set of security controls have been implemented. |
| **Tier 2** | Represents a higher level of cybersecurity capability than Tier 1. |
| **Tier 3** | Represents a higher level of cybersecurity capability than Tier 2. |
| **Tier 4** | The highest level, representing an advanced and adaptive cybersecurity approach. |

Tiers help organizations:

- Assess their security posture.
- Measure cybersecurity performance.
- Identify areas for improvement.

Tiers are not simply a **yes/no** measurement. They provide a range that helps organizations understand what is and is not working within their security programs.

## Profiles

**CSF Profiles** are templates based on the NIST CSF that can be tailored to address the specific risks of an organization or industry.

Profiles can be used to:

- Develop a baseline for cybersecurity plans.
- Represent the current state of an organization's security posture.
- Compare the current cybersecurity posture with a specific industry standard.
- Identify security improvements.

Profiles can be thought of as snapshots of a security plan at a particular point in time. Comparing profiles from different points in time can provide insight into how an organization's security posture has changed.

## Implementing the NIST CSF

**Compliance** measures how well an organization adheres to internal standards and external regulations.

Organizations may choose to use the NIST CSF to help achieve compliance with various regulations.

> **Note:** The NIST CSF is voluntary. Regulations, in contrast, are requirements that must be followed.

The CSF can be challenging to implement because of its level of detail. Organizations may also be unsure how the framework fits with their existing security plans or where to begin when developing a new plan.

The **Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA)** provides guidance for implementing the NIST CSF.

### Recommended Implementation Process

A general implementation process includes:

1. **Create a Current Profile**
   - Document the current state of security operations.
   - Identify the specific security needs of the business.

2. **Perform a Risk Assessment**
   - Identify which current operations meet business and regulatory requirements.
   - Assess existing cybersecurity risks.

3. **Analyze and Prioritize Gaps**
   - Identify gaps in security operations.
   - Prioritize gaps that place business assets at risk.

4. **Implement a Plan of Action**
   - Develop and implement actions that support the organization's security goals and objectives.

> **Pro Tip:** Consider current **risk, threat, and vulnerability trends** when using the NIST CSF.

## Industries Using the NIST CSF

The NIST CSF has continued to evolve since its introduction in 2014.

Its design is influenced by security standards and best practices used by organizations around the world.

The framework provides several benefits:

- It is flexible across industries.
- It aligns with security practices used by many organizations.
- It can help organizations address regulatory compliance requirements.
- It can support security requirements shared between business partners.

## Key Takeaways

- **Compliance** is the process of adhering to internal standards and external regulations.
- Compliance helps organizations maintain trust, reputation, safety, and data integrity while reducing financial and legal risks.
- **Regulations** are mandatory rules established by governments or other authorities.
- The **NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)** is a voluntary framework for managing cybersecurity risk.
- The NIST CSF consists of three main components: **Core, Tiers, and Profiles**.
- The current CSF Core has six functions: **Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover, and Govern**.
- **Tiers** measure the sophistication of an organization's cybersecurity program on a scale from 1 to 4.
- **Profiles** provide tailored views and baselines for an organization's cybersecurity posture.
- CISA provides guidance for implementing the NIST CSF.
- A general implementation process involves creating a current profile, performing a risk assessment, prioritizing security gaps, and implementing a plan of action.
- The NIST CSF is flexible and can be used across different industries and organizations.
