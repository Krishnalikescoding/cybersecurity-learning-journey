# Data Lifecycle and Data Governance

## Overview

Organizations handle large amounts of data that must be kept private and secure. Data can be vulnerable whether it is:

- **At rest**
- **In use**
- **In transit**

Regardless of its state, information should be protected by limiting access and authorization.

The **data lifecycle** provides a model for understanding how data moves through an organization and how security controls can be applied at each stage. These controls help maintain the **Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability (CIA)** of information.

## Data Lifecycle

**Data Lifecycle:** A model that describes how data flows through an organization from the moment it is created until it is no longer useful.

The data lifecycle has five general stages:

1. **Collect**
2. **Store**
3. **Use**
4. **Archive**
5. **Destroy**

### 1. Collect

Data is gathered from internal and external sources.

### 2. Store

Collected data is stored so that it can be accessed and used when needed.

### 3. Use

Data is accessed, processed, or used by authorized users and systems.

### 4. Archive

Data that is no longer actively used can be archived for future reference or access.

### 5. Destroy

Data that is no longer useful is destroyed.

> **Important:** Information should be protected throughout every stage of the data lifecycle. It should remain accessible and recoverable if something goes wrong.

## Data Governance

Organizations collect and manage massive amounts of data from many different sources. A structured approach is necessary to keep this information private, accurate, available, and secure.

**Data Governance:** A set of processes that defines how an organization manages information.

Data governance commonly includes policies that specify how data should be:

- Kept private
- Maintained accurately
- Kept available
- Protected throughout its lifecycle

Effective data governance is a collaborative process involving different roles and responsibilities.

## Data Governance Roles

Data governance policies commonly define three important roles:

### Data Owner

**Data Owner:** The person who decides who can access, edit, use, or destroy their information.

The data owner is responsible for making decisions about how their information is accessed and handled.

### Data Custodian

**Data Custodian:** Anyone or anything responsible for the safe handling, transport, and storage of information.

Data custodians can include:

- People
- Organizations
- Systems

As a data custodian, a security professional is primarily responsible for maintaining the organization's security and privacy rules.

### Data Steward

**Data Steward:** The person or group responsible for maintaining and implementing an organization's data governance policies.

## Data Governance and Security

Organizations use many IT systems to:

- Store data
- Move data
- Transform data

Data governance policies assign accountability to **data owners, data custodians, and data stewards**.

Security professionals play an important role in data governance. As data custodians, they help ensure that information is not:

- Damaged
- Stolen
- Misused

## Data Governance Policy

A **data governance policy** is a policy within an organization's security plan that defines how information should be managed.

These policies:

- Define procedures for keeping data safe.
- Limit who or what can access data.
- Establish responsibilities for protecting information.
- Guide how data should be managed throughout its lifecycle.

## Legally Protected Information

Data can represent many aspects of a person's life, including:

- Personal thoughts
- Actions
- Choices
- Purchases
- Medical decisions

Because of this, data owners should have control over whether their data is shared.

Security professionals must respect data privacy decisions and protect personal information from unauthorized use and disclosure.

## Types of Protected Information

Organizations may handle different types of personal information that require additional protection.

### Personally Identifiable Information (PII)

**Personally Identifiable Information (PII):** Information that can be used to infer an individual's identity.

PII can include information that can be used to:

- Contact someone
- Locate someone

### Protected Health Information (PHI)

**Protected Health Information (PHI):** Information relating to an individual's past, present, or future physical or mental health or condition.

In the United States, PHI is regulated by the **Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)**.

In the European Union, similar health information is regulated under the **General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)**.

### Sensitive Personally Identifiable Information (SPII)

**Sensitive Personally Identifiable Information (SPII)** is a specific type of PII that requires stricter handling.

The **S** in SPII stands for **sensitive**.

SPII should generally only be accessed on a **need-to-know basis**.

Examples include:

- Bank account numbers
- Login credentials

## PII vs PHI vs SPII

| Type | Meaning | Examples / Context |
|---|---|---|
| **PII** | Information that can be used to infer an individual's identity or contact or locate them. | Personal identifying information |
| **PHI** | Information related to an individual's physical or mental health or condition. | Health information regulated by HIPAA in the U.S. |
| **SPII** | A sensitive type of PII requiring stricter handling and need-to-know access. | Bank account numbers, login credentials |

## Protecting Data Throughout Its Lifecycle

Data owners may generate more information than they can directly manage. As a result, data custodians and stewards may sometimes lack explicit instructions for handling specific types of data.

Government and regulatory agencies help address this by establishing requirements for protecting certain types of information.

Organizations should protect personal information from:

- Unauthorized access
- Unauthorized use
- Unauthorized disclosure

Security controls and data governance policies should support appropriate protection throughout the entire data lifecycle.

## Key Takeaways

- The **data lifecycle** describes how data moves through an organization.
- The five stages are **Collect, Store, Use, Archive, and Destroy**.
- Data should remain protected, accessible, and recoverable throughout its lifecycle.
- **Data governance** defines how an organization manages information.
- The three key data governance roles are **data owner, data custodian, and data steward**.
- **Data owners** decide who can access, edit, use, or destroy information.
- **Data custodians** are responsible for the safe handling, transport, and storage of information.
- **Data stewards** maintain and implement data governance policies.
- **PII**, **PHI**, and **SPII** are types of personal information that require appropriate protection.
- **SPII** requires stricter handling and should generally be accessed on a need-to-know basis.
- Protecting data throughout its lifecycle helps organizations maintain the **confidentiality, integrity, and availability (CIA)** of information.
